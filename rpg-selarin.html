A traveler’s introduction

Welcome to Selarin

Selarin is a world of established kingdoms, working farms, crowded ports, and roads that pass things no living scholar can explain. A ruler may claim a throne older than the surviving chronicles; a village may hold its market beside a stone that has never weathered.

In 742 AR, the major realms are at peace, but frontier raids, disputed tolls, and old territorial claims still bring conflict. The stories below reflect knowledge taught by chroniclers, temples, and travelers. Where traditions disagree, that uncertainty is part of the world.

This guide presents player-facing world information, history, and publicly known lore.

Above a ruined city, a chamber may still hang in the air, with no stair, door, or spell known to reach it. Most journeys remain ordinary, but beyond the maintained roads lie creatures, old workings, and mysteries that reward a careful adventurer.

Beneath Memory

Before about 9000 BR

Creation traditions, the oldest gods, and stories of the Deep Heart

Explore this age

No account of Selarin's beginning can be checked against a surviving mortal record. The oldest hymns describe a world still taking shape, where earth had not settled and the first forests repeatedly vanished beneath fire, frost, or sea. Some tell of gods making order. Others place the gods within a world already alive.

The stories of the Deep Heart

Across otherwise unrelated traditions appears the image of something immense beneath the earth. Mountain songs call it the First Sleeper. River prayers speak of a heart too large for the sky. Later scholars gathered these stories under the name Deep Heart, although they disagree about whether the term describes a being, a natural power, or a poetic explanation of the Long Breath. In one familiar tale, Ilyra spreads roots over the earth while Ordan gives the mountains their foundations. Thessar refuses to still the winds, insisting that growing things must have movement and room to change. Their bargain creates a world that can endure without becoming motionless. It is often recited when a community plants a new orchard or raises a boundary stone. Malrec's temples preserve a different telling. They credit a first sovereign who commanded the unruly earth to submit. Worshippers of Ilyra answer that no command can make a field fertile forever. Neither tradition supplies proof of what lies beneath the land, but each has influenced how rulers understand their obligations to it.

The first divine quarrels

The oldest surviving religious traditions name Ilyra, Ordan, Thessar, and Sovan among the powers present before organized mortal kingdoms. Sovan appears as the keeper of the first passage between life and death. In the Tale of the Unburied Morning, he leads the dead away so that grief does not hold the living in an endless day. His shrines still leave a door or window open during a funeral. Mereth's mercy and Nharos's hunger enter the stories when living creatures begin to suffer. Mereth tends a wounded beast that can offer her neither prayer nor repayment. Nharos consumes what others need and calls survival his only law. Their conflict remains central to disputes over healing, famine relief, and the use of another person's life to extend one's own. Avariel's earliest legend concerns a judge who places her own seat beneath the law she announces. Malrec calls that act a surrender of rightful power. Varkhul appears in tales of destruction that outlast its cause, while Drazha is the pursuer who continues the hunt after hunger is satisfied. These stories provide moral language rather than a reliable order of divine births.

What remains of the age

Unnaturally warm valleys, strange seams of stone, and deep places that trouble sleepers are sometimes attributed to these first events. Natural causes, older magic, and religious explanations compete. Even the longest dwarven genealogies stop far short of this age. Its importance lies in the customs it established: burial as a duty, rule as an obligation, and the belief that the land has rhythms no crown can wholly command.

Creatures in the oldest stories

The oldest mountain songs speak of dragons already old when the peaks first rose, of vast serpents beneath tidal waters, and of giants who measured their steps by the shaping of the land. Scholars do not claim that such stories describe a single forgotten kingdom. They do note that some living creatures thrive in places of unusual heat, deep stone, and enduring magic. To a traveler today, the lesson is simple: the oldest wilderness may shelter life that no familiar bestiary fully explains.

First Peoples

About 9000 to 4200 BR

Early realms, the First Courts, and the first surviving accounts of ascension

Explore this age

The earliest recoverable histories concern communities that learned where they could remain. Families settled river terraces, sheltered coasts, and mountain valleys. They kept observations of harvests and animal migrations, passing them through song before committing them to bark, clay, and stone. These peoples were diverse from the beginning. Later claims that any one ancestry alone built civilization tell us more about royal ambition than ancient life.

The First Courts

The societies now grouped together as the First Courts developed an unusually close relationship with living magic. Some grew halls from trees without killing them. Others preserved testimony in groves or established roads whose course depended on remembered agreements. Their courts were separate communities, often rivals, rather than provinces of one empire. Elven lineages preserved much of their ceremonial language, which explains Elaran's strong connection to their legacy. Yet surviving images show many peoples attending those courts. Hospitality, inheritance, and the right to alter a landscape were persistent concerns. A dispute about cutting timber might involve the needs of a village, a lord's claim, and promises made by grandparents to a place they considered alive.

Nivara and the Open Song

Nivara is remembered as a mortal elven singer attached to a court that controlled who could hear its ancestral songs. When exiles were refused the music used to mourn their dead, she carried it beyond the court and taught it in a common tongue. Her punishment, according to the best-known account, was the surrender of her voice. At the exiles' next funeral, every listener heard her sing, though she stood silent. Performers in distant settlements reported the same song that night. The oldest surviving shrines to Nivara date from the following generations. Her mortal existence and later divinity are broadly accepted; whether the miracle marked her ascension or revealed a change already completed remains disputed. Today, Nivara's festivals begin with a performance offered without payment. Some temples teach that inspiration carries an obligation to share. Others emphasize the freedom of an artist to refuse a patron. Elaran's courts honor her while still arguing about which traditions should remain private.

Roads between strangers

Trade carried metal, salt, seeds, and stories between early settlements. Leth became a favored patron of travelers who survived uncertain roads. In the Tale of the Wrong Turning, a caravan misses its destination and discovers a valley able to feed a starving town. The story's oldest versions never identify Leth as mortal; later claimants to his ancestry have produced little agreement. Sereva's worship also appears in early accounts of succession disputes. Her tale of the Borrowed Face concerns a trusted adviser who turns each member of a household against the others without speaking a single outright lie. The story helped establish the belief that truthful words can still serve betrayal. By the age's end, fortified towns, durable trade routes, and ambitious schools had appeared. The First Courts continued in some regions, but growing populations demanded forms of cooperation that personal memory and inherited custom could no longer reliably supply.

Pacts with the living world

The First Courts remembered more neighbors than the communities now called civilized. Songs describe giant eagles carrying messages across high passes, watchful unicorns at protected springs, and forest guardians who accepted gifts or kept their distance according to old agreements. Some tales are ceremony rather than literal history. Yet the habit of asking a woodland keeper before cutting ancient trees survives, especially in Elaran, and travelers still leave particular groves undisturbed.

Great Works

About 4200 to 1300 BR

The Concord of Towers, Covenant Cities, and wonders beyond modern mastery

Explore this age

Selarin's ancient civilizations reached their greatest material achievements during this long age. Their learning accumulated across generations and crossed political boundaries. They could build things modern practitioners can describe only in fragments. They were also dependent on institutions, trained workers, and agreements whose loss would eventually make those achievements impossible to maintain.

Towers and covenant cities

The Concord of Towers began as an association of observatories and schools. Shared measurements allowed its members to coordinate distant workings, then to build gateways, suspended chambers, and instruments that influenced local weather. It never governed every land, and its member cities frequently disagreed. Its greatest accomplishment was making cooperation seem more profitable than secrecy for a time. The Covenant Cities pursued another tradition: binding a lasting principle into a place. Protected burial grounds, halls without shadows, and sanctuaries governed by unusual rules survive from their age. They exchanged knowledge with the Concord and the surviving First Courts. The three traditions overlapped, borrowed freely, and sometimes claimed one another's achievements.

Veyra and the Unaltered Record

Veyra was a human recorder serving a council of covenant cities. When its rulers demanded that she amend a treaty to erase the rights of a defeated neighbor, she instead read the original before their assembled witnesses. Copies later appeared in archives too far apart for any ordinary messenger to have carried them. Her cult dates her ascension to that public reading. Rival accounts describe years of persecution before she vanished from mortal life. Both agree on the part she played: preserving an obligation that powerful people wished to make disappear. Her priests now distinguish carefully between recording a claim and proving it true. Depositing a document in her temple does not make its contents sacred fact.

The square at Veycross

The Witness Court predates the modern city surrounding it. Within its original stone boundary, people cannot knowingly speak a falsehood. They may remain silent, speak narrowly, or repeat something mistaken that they honestly believe. The square does not force anyone to keep a promise after leaving it. Veyra's followers became its best-known custodians. Avariel's judges regarded it as an aid to justice, while other priests warned that exact words could conceal an unjust bargain. Professional questioners and public readers developed around the court. Its original builders and intended purpose remain uncertain, despite confident claims made by several faiths.

An inheritance beyond present reach

Ordan's temples supported standards of measure and the obligations of builders. Ilyra's servants kept seasonal records. Mereth's communities established houses of healing along the routes. Their cooperation was substantial, but prosperity was uneven: some districts enjoyed controlled harvests while others supplied labor or materials on harsh terms. Silent gates and unreachable structures remain across Selarin. A gateway may lack its distant counterpart; a weather tower may depend on instruments lost centuries ago. Some wonders still function but offer no known means of control. Even excellent modern magic cannot simply replace the societies that made them. Many waystones are also demonstrably ancient, though no accepted chronology explains the whole of their construction.

The enduring guardians

The Concord of Towers shaped more than stone. Records describe tireless sentinels at bridges, enchanted armor in public halls, and laboring constructs that held back rivers or lifted impossible weight. Certain keeps maintained trained griffons, while ambitious artificers attempted to breed or bind creatures suited to dangerous work. The greatest projects are lost, but a silent guardian at an abandoned gate may still obey an instruction that nobody remembers issuing. The wiser antiquarian treats a beautiful ruin as a place with rules, not an empty quarry.

Disagreement

About 1300 to 420 BR

Rival claims to magical prosperity, religious divisions, and failed settlements

Explore this age

The disputes that ended the ancient order began while much of it still prospered. Cities disagreed over access to gateways, the cost of maintaining great works, and who could change conditions shared with their neighbors. Records describe accusations that one realm's favorable weather had worsened another's harvest. Sometimes the evidence was compelling; sometimes a failed crop became a convenient reason to threaten a rival.

Cevrane and the Ledger of Winter

Cevrane first appears as a mortal human financier who supplied grain after a succession of white summers. He offered food against future labor, land, and inheritance. A family might survive one winter only to discover that its descendants remained liable for the price. His contracts acquired a terrible reputation for surviving every attempt to destroy or evade them. At the event called the Closing of Accounts, witnesses reported his signature burning into ledgers in several cities at once. His agents began receiving answers to prayers addressed to him. His followers celebrate a mortal who made obligation endure beyond death; his opponents commemorate the same event as the elevation of exploitation into divine authority. Cevrane remains a lesser god, but his institutions reach far beyond any single throne.

Caelor and the Breaking of the Chain

Caelor was a mortal leader among communities bound to hereditary service. The oldest credible accounts agree that he refused a settlement offering freedom only to his own household. His followers seized the registers by which their service was enforced and held the western causeways long enough for entire districts to leave. At the final crossing, Caelor disappeared into a storm. Survivors described a light moving against the wind and chains opening without a hand upon them. Shrines established by those survivors became centers of an enduring faith. Caelor's recognition as a greater power came over subsequent centuries as his worship spread. His ascension did not instantly end bondage, and his churches still debate when rebellion becomes a duty.

The Last Concord

Representatives of several traditions attempted one final broad settlement late in the age. Fragmentary records call it the Last Concord. Delegates agreed to common measures, restrictions on certain workings, and shared obligations that surviving copies describe only incompletely. Ordan's and Avariel's temples honor the attempt; Malrec's theologians argue that a single sovereign could have enforced what a council could not. The agreement preserved peace in some places. Elsewhere, rulers withheld resources, altered its terms, or denied that their predecessors had possessed authority to bind them. What its most ambitious projects accomplished remains disputed. Modern claims to have recovered a complete account have repeatedly failed scholarly scrutiny.

Faith and faction

Thessar's worshippers resisted efforts to suppress every unpredictable change. Nharos offered stolen vitality to patrons afraid of losing their wealth or years. Varkhul's cults encouraged destruction as an answer to humiliation. Drazha's followers supplied hunters to regimes pursuing fugitives. Sereva's agents made reconciliation harder by exposing genuine wrongs at moments calculated to provoke revenge. The age ended with enough knowledge to build wonders, but too little trust to keep many of them working together.

When the wilds changed

As rival courts competed for magical advantage, some experiments altered the creatures around them. Accounts name manticores and chimeric predators along disputed borders, and describe valleys where familiar beasts no longer behaved in familiar ways. Every alarming animal was not the product of a wizard's ambition; pamphleteers often accused their enemies without proof. Nonetheless, the period left a long tradition of warded roads, strict rules for transporting dangerous specimens, and warnings about abandoned laboratories.

Sundering Years

420 to 1 BR

Collapsing networks, migrations, lost cities, and enduring acts of rescue

Explore this age

The Sundering was a succession of failures and wars extending over four centuries. Its conventional beginning marks the closure of several important gateways within one generation. Their loss disrupted food supplies and military alliances. Later disasters struck societies already struggling to repair the first damage. Some realms fell quickly; others survived long enough to receive refugees from places their grandchildren knew only by name.

Teren and the Seven Watches

Teren was a dwarven captain charged with a Crownspine passage during an evacuation. When his employers ordered the gates shut against foreign refugees, he opened the storehouses and kept the route clear. The account of the Seven Watches describes a defense maintained through successive assaults and a collapse that buried the approaches. The final refugees said that Teren, already mortally wounded, remained at the gate after his companions had withdrawn. Later travelers found the passage standing and received aid from an armored figure bearing his broken shield. His worship took root among soldiers, smiths, and bridge keepers of many peoples. Avariel's church honors him as a divine companion whose service outranks obedience to an unjust command. Khardun still treats the maintenance of safe passage as a public duty. Soldiers elsewhere swear by Teren before escorting civilians, although commanders have also invoked him to justify hopeless defenses that served no one. His priests consider the distinction essential.

Elsira and the House of Open Doors

Elsira kept a roadside house in country overwhelmed by winter refugees. The most widespread tradition describes her as a halfling widow, though later paintings often give her the appearance of their own community. Her household accepted strangers until its stores were exhausted. When armed men demanded the surrender of people sheltering inside, she barred the door against them and continued admitting those fleeing the cold through the kitchen. Survivors remembered rooms that held more people than the building should have allowed. After Elsira's death, travelers began finding the same open doorway in distant storms. Accounts from separate refugee communities established her cult. Her ascension is widely accepted, while the particulars remain a cherished subject of local storytelling. Inns dedicated to her reserve a place for someone who cannot pay.

What could be saved

Mereth's healers and Sovan's funerary orders often traveled together. One tended survivors while the other recorded the dead, helping displaced families recover names and property claims. Veyra's archivists carried copies into mountain vaults. Nivara's performers preserved histories in forms that could travel without books. Their efforts explain why fragments endured even where institutions collapsed. Claims that gods themselves were wounded or slain also belong to these centuries. Ruined sanctuaries name powers absent from the modern pantheon, but accounts disagree over their identities and fates. Possessing an old divine title is not proof of having inherited its power. By the Sundering's end, surviving communities had established new roads and local authorities. At Veycross, envoys negotiated safe passage, recognized certain refugee claims, and agreed on measures for trade. Their settlement became the starting point of the Accord Reckoning. It restored particular relationships; peace across the entire continent took much longer.

The roads reclaimed

When maintained wards, bridges, and patrols failed, wolves and larger predators followed abandoned livestock through empty country. Trolls settled beneath neglected crossings; wyverns returned to cliffs once watched by garrisons; and some battlefields became places where the dead did not rest easily. Survivors learned to travel in groups, mark safe shelters, and distinguish a ruined village from a ruin still defended by ancient workings. Many customs dismissed as superstition began as practical answers to real dangers.

Inheritance

1 AR to the present year 742 AR

The modern realms, renewed trade, and a world with its own ambitions

Explore this age

The modern kingdoms grew through seven centuries of recovery, rivalry, and invention. They possess histories of their own, with institutions that respond to current needs. Most people regard ancient ruins with a mixture of curiosity and caution. A repaired mill or dependable bridge usually matters more to a community than a lost wonder no one can operate.

The making of the modern realms

Elaran's surviving forest courts renewed their mutual obligations in 63 AR. Their compact recognized stewardship over watersheds and forests while limiting the authority of any one court. The Crownspine holds established Khardun's present crown in 118 AR, accepting common defenses and standards for roads without surrendering their assemblies. Caldris consolidated around coastal lords and chartered market towns in 204 AR. Its crown gained recognition partly by securing the southern route around the mountains. Aramere's River Compact of 287 AR bound several principalities to shared granaries and flood works. Its monarchs still defend their legitimacy through those obligations. Vardane's northern assemblies recognized a common crown in 351 AR after years of disputes over pasture and fortified crossings. The Serathian imperial settlement followed in 426 AR, bringing the lower Auren and several eastern ports under one administration. Serath's rulers claimed ancient continuity, but the state they constructed depended on new bargains with cities and provincial elites.

The wars that fixed the borders

The Pass Disputes of 512 to 519 AR established many of Khardun's toll rights, although the interpretation of those rights still changes at inconvenient moments. Fighting on Elaran's southern edge produced the Woodland Articles of 558 AR, recognizing some protected forests while leaving disputed settlements outside any clear agreement. The Grain War between Aramere and Serath lasted from 603 to 607 AR. Both sides suffered from interrupted trade, and neither gained the victory it had promised. The Auren Navigation Accord limited certain duties and established procedures for inspecting grain barges. Arguments now usually reach negotiators before armies, though merchants continue to fund armed escorts. The Kharzai Dominion took its present form in 668 AR, when a military priesthood consolidated several plateau fortresses. Frontier clashes with Vardane and uneasy trade with Serath have continued since. It remains a smaller power, dangerous chiefly where terrain and discipline let it choose the terms of a confrontation.

Living in the present

The six major realms are not presently at war. Border incidents still occur, and peace depends on arrangements that require continual attention. Grain moves downriver, metals cross the mountain passes, and ports exchange goods that inland communities cannot produce. Temples, kinship, and commerce create loyalties that cross political lines. Observers report unfamiliar migrations and scattered episodes of Breathweather, but disagree about whether they mark a larger change. Ancient inscriptions still attract scholars. New settlements grow beside old roads. Selarin offers room for ordinary ambition, generous deeds, and difficult choices without requiring every traveler to understand the world's oldest mysteries.

A world shared with wonders

Modern rulers cannot put every griffon, giant, or wyvern beyond their borders. Instead, they fund road watches, record sightings, and sometimes negotiate safe passage around known territories. Markets trade in alchemical reagents, feathers, hides, and protective charms, although counterfeit goods are common. Village bells may warn of a dangerous beast just as readily as fire or flood. Most journeys remain ordinary; what makes adventurers valuable is their willingness to travel where ordinary precautions are no longer enough.

Life and ancient mysteries

The Long Breath & the waystones

Seasons, magic, and ancient landmarks shape everyday travel as much as crowns and borders do.

Seasons and the Long Breath

Spring, summer, autumn, and winter ordinarily follow familiar patterns. Beneath them, observers recognize a slower, uneven rhythm called the Long Breath. Over decades or centuries, forests grow more vigorously, magical creatures alter their migrations, and places once rich in ambient magic become quieter. Some regions remain consistently more magical than others. Breathweather is the common name for unusual disturbances associated with these changes. Snow may fall during summer. Warm winter winds may release dangerous floods. A crownstorm can turn for days over inland country, while tremors change springs or close a mountain road. Most communities experience long intervals of ordinary weather between serious events. Agriculture and travel remain possible because catastrophe is exceptional.

What an ordinary traveler knows

Magic is a fact of life, although access varies greatly. A Serathian city supports academies and licensed practitioners; a remote Vardane valley may depend on one shrine keeper and an occasional visiting healer. A region with little ambient magic does not routinely prevent a wizard from casting spells or a priest from receiving divine aid. Ancient waystones stand across the realms. Their surfaces scarcely show the passage of time, and ordinary tools cannot cut them. People build roads, shrines, and markets around them. Their makers and purpose remain matters of argument. A traveler is more likely to hear a local custom about a stone than a convincing explanation of it.

Creatures along the road

In settled country, sightings of monstrous creatures are noteworthy rather than routine. Local watches collect credible reports of griffons, trolls, owlbears, and other hazards; experienced carriers know which streams, nesting cliffs, and stretches of road merit caution. A market may sell griffon feathers or warding tokens without having a griffon anywhere near its walls.

Faith and traditions

Sacred history

In the oldest judgment tale, Avariel places her own seat beneath the law she announces. Mortal rulers inherit obligations, not exemptions. Her temples supported fair hearings in the Covenant Cities and later recognized Teren's defense of refugees as sacred service. Her image often shows an unworn crown: authority must first demonstrate that it deserves to be carried.

Teachings

Hear those who lack powerful advocates. Apply the standards you demand of others. Protect people while establishing the truth, and repair the harm caused by your own judgments.

Worship and clergy

Judges, advocates, watch officers, and ordinary petitioners visit her open courts. The Order of the Uncrowned Bench offers mediation and representation in Caldris and Serath. Its clergy reject trial by wealth or birth, but disagree about when resistance to an unjust government becomes lawful. Before hearing a case, a priest places a personal token beside the petitioner's to acknowledge equal accountability.

Observance

At the annual Day of Open Petition, temples hear grievances without an entry fee and publicly account for their own decisions.

Divine relationships

She honors Teren and works readily with Veyra. She opposes Malrec's claim that strength creates legitimacy and Cevrane's use of lawful words to shelter injustice.

Sacred history

During the Sundering, the dwarven captain Teren kept a Crownspine passage open after receiving orders to shut out refugees. Accounts of the Seven Watches end with his mortal wounds and later appearances at the surviving gate. His worship spread beyond dwarven holds because the people he protected came from many lands. Teachings

Know whom your oath protects. Maintain the equipment and routes upon which others depend. Never confuse obedience with the discharge of your duty.

Worship and clergy

The Wardens of the Seven Watches serve as escorts, armorers, rescue workers, and shrine guards. Khardun's bridge crews often include a lay devotee responsible for inspecting neglected repairs. Vardane's sentries invoke him before winter patrols. Clergy bless an object only after checking that it can do its job; polishing unsafe armor is no substitute for mending it.

Observance

The Seven Watches are marked locally by a night of shared guard duty, with each relief feeding and thanking the people it replaces.

Divine relationships

Avariel is his closest divine ally. Elsira's shelters rely on his guardians. He opposes Malrec when commands sacrifice dependents merely to preserve a superior's prestige.

Faith and traditions

Sacred history

Mereth's earliest story begins with a wounded beast that can offer neither gratitude nor repayment. She tends it anyway. Her healing houses crossed ancient borders, and during the Sundering her servants worked beside Sovan's record keepers. Her faith measures mercy by the suffering relieved, not by the number of converts gained. Renewal can mean recovery, reconciliation, or a peaceful end when healing is no longer possible.

Teachings

Offer care without demanding devotion. Respect the person receiving help. Ease suffering without stealing someone else's life to postpone an ending.

Worship and clergy

The Houses of the Sheltered Drop maintain infirmaries, traveling aid wagons, and gardens of medicinal plants. They are prominent in Aramere and the larger cities. Healing is not inexhaustible; clergy must allocate scarce supplies and train ordinary carers. Some accept military protection, while others fear that a garrison will turn their sanctuary into a strategic prize. Patients are offered clean water before questions about payment.

Observance

The First Cup is a household rite: the first clean water drawn after a hardship is shared with someone still recovering.

Divine relationships

She cooperates with Elsira and Sovan. Nharos is her enduring enemy. She disputes doctrines that treat sickness as proof that a sufferer deserves abandonment.

Faith and traditions

Sacred history

Elsira was a halfling innkeeper whose house sheltered refugees during the Sundering. When armed pursuers demanded entry, she barred their way while continuing to admit people fleeing the cold. Survivors recalled rooms larger than the building allowed. After her death, travelers found her doorway in distant storms. Her followers preserve many local versions, united by the conviction that hospitality requires courage as well as generosity.

Teachings

Make shelter visible to those who need it. Do not surrender a guest to cruelty. A host's generosity does not grant ownership of a guest's future.

Worship and clergy

The Fellowship of Open Doors links inns, shrines, and emergency shelters without imposing one household rule everywhere. Mother Hessa Vale coordinates traveling stewards. Vardane's winter houses and Aramere's road refuges are especially important. Clergy keep practical stores, negotiate safe passage, and insist that guests must not endanger one another. An empty chair marks welcome; it is not reserved for a wealthy patron who never arrives.

Observance

On the Lantern Evening before the first expected hard frost, communities inspect shelters, replenish fuel, and leave a light at the door.

Divine relationships

Mereth tends those her houses receive, and Teren protects the threshold. She opposes Cevrane when shelter becomes a debt used to trap its recipient.

Sacred history

Caelor led communities bound to hereditary service during the Age of Disagreement. He refused an offer that would free only his household, then held the western causeways while others escaped. At the final crossing he vanished into a storm; witnesses saw light moving against the wind and chains opening. His worship later grew into that of a greater god. Do not purchase your freedom with another's captivity. Help people choose their own future. Challenge domination without making yourself its replacement.

Worship and clergy

The Dawn Companions organize as local circles rather than a single command. Some buy out coercive debts; others shelter fugitives, petition rulers, or resist violent captors. They are strongest along trade routes and among displaced communities. Their disagreements concern methods, especially when a revolt could expose dependents to retaliation. An open hand, offered without grasping the recipient, accompanies their blessings.

Observance

At the Dawn of Release, each circle commemorates a local liberation and undertakes a useful task requested by those who were freed.

He opposes Malrec and Cevrane. Nivara shares his defense of expression. Avariel's followers often support his aims while disputing the legitimacy of his methods.

Foreign observers describe a severe and calculating ruler whose decrees are carried out by generals, temple officers, and appointed magistrates. His authority is formidable, although no single official governs every settlement without local resistance.

The Dominion enforces rigid hierarchy and compulsory labor, rewarding service that strengthens its fortresses and supply lines. Hobgoblins remain prominent among its military officers, but its inhabitants include many ancestries, subject communities, and dissenters who reject the ruling institutions' ambitions. Its government is not the inevitable character of any people. Border merchants may encounter skilled smiths, cautious negotiators, and families more concerned with surviving the next winter than spreading their ruler's power.

Terraced cultivation, hardy livestock, reservoirs, and mineral trade support the strongholds. Their rulers seek secure water and defensible access to better land, avoiding open campaigns where larger neighbors could bring their full resources to bear. The Blackglass heights shelter ash drakes, wyverns, and dangerous creatures drawn to warm fissures; patrol orders treat such hazards as seriously as enemy scouts. Serathian intermediaries offer markets, while Vardane maintains an uneasy mixture of confrontation and negotiation. The Dominion's stonewardens forbid interference with local waystones, which they regard as sacred monuments of restraint and authority.

The city

Aurenhall rises beside the Auren in a succession of stone terraces. Roofed stairs connect the busy lower quays with the council halls above. Grain dust whitens the dock railings; in the upper streets, messengers weave between petitioners carrying rolled plans and packets of provincial seals. River bells announce changing water levels as well as the hour. Population: approximately 76,000. Humans form the largest community, with longstanding halfling neighborhoods, dwarven engineering families, and residents of many other ancestries. Household, occupation, and district shape daily life more strongly than ancestry alone.

Government and leadership

King Edric Vaun and the River Council govern the realm here. The city's own services fall to Lord Provost Ansel Dorr and a council of ward representatives. Ansel, a graying human former granary clerk, regards refuse collection and safe stairs as matters of royal dignity. He resents courtiers who promise public celebrations without paying for the cleanup. The provost controls ordinary permits, street maintenance, and the watch budget. Crown guards answer to a separate command. A petitioner seeking justice against a royal contractor may therefore need both civic records and a crown hearing.

Defense and commerce

The civic watch patrols in shifts, supported by separate crown guards and a river patrol. Walls protect the upper approaches; river gates and raised storehouses secure valuable cargo. During floods, rescue and evacuation take priority over routine patrols. Grain brokerage, milling, ropework, cart building, legal services, and government spending support the city. Ordinary equipment is plentiful. Specialist magic requires an appointment, a willing practitioner, and payment; the presence of temples does not make powerful services routinely available.

Districts and landmarks

The Crown Terrace contains the royal residence, administrative courts, and formal gardens. Beneath it, the Council Steps are lined with copying shops, inexpensive food stalls, and lodgings rented by provincial petitioners. Lower Auren is a working district of warehouses, boatyards, and tightly packed homes. The Compact House. This broad hall houses the River Council and public copies of its resolutions. Clerk-Marshal Jessa Orr, a precise half-elf with an excellent memory for neglected petitions, manages access. Visitors can inspect posted decisions; private provincial correspondence requires permission. The Seven Granaries. Seven immense stores stand on raised foundations with separate access yards. Warden-General Tomas Reed's office is here. The oldest outer wall carries flood marks from several centuries. A public reserve tally is updated at each market week, attracting anyone with reasons to distrust grain prices. The Measure Stair. A long riverside stair bears metal height marks maintained by the Bankwardens. Its lowest flights disappear in high water. Della Marr's crews use it for training, and families gather on the upper landing when the bells warn of a rising river.

Organizations and places to stay

The League of Bread and Mill. Led by Master Miller Oren Vask, this trade association negotiates delivery schedules and investigates adulterated flour. Bakers accuse its mill owners of protecting one another; Oren argues that public panic can ruin honest households before an inquiry begins. Its hall in Lower Auren accepts written complaints and commissions independent sampling. The House of the Sheltered Drop. Senior healer Adra Fen directs Mereth's principal public hospice. Donations fund ordinary care and convalescent beds. She needs carriers, clean supplies, and assistance reaching patients; she will not sell priority in an emergency to a noble household. The Reed Crown. Innkeeper Pella Norr rents quiet rooms near the Council Steps and serves a communal supper where provincial rivals must share the same bread. Her noticeboard lists clerical work, escort requests, and rooms available to petitioners with modest means.

Primary faiths

The Lower Bench offers mediation in Lower Auren; the Last Passage House tends funerals and family registers outside the busiest wards. Both welcome ordinary households as well as officials.

Connections beyond the settlement

The Granary Trust directs inspectors from the Seven Granaries to Fairmead and Dunmere, while western inspections pass through Threeways. The Bankwardens share gauge reports with Dunmere and Northbridge. An Auren Factors countinghouse handles river contracts here; its local member houses answer commercially to Speaker Mara Venn, not to the River Council.

Bargemen, warehouse workers, merchants, and families displaced by earlier floods give the city a mixed population. Several thousand seasonal hands arrive during the busiest grain shipments.

Government and leadership

Countess Selene Harrow holds the crown's local commission; elected aldermen manage the markets and neighborhood services. Selene, a composed human in her forties, has an injured knee from an old quay collapse and conducts many meetings while walking slowly along the docks. She insists on seeing the work for which she is asked to pay. Harbormaster Iven Sarr controls berth assignments and channel notices. He is a broad-shouldered half-orc who knows which captains can move a damaged vessel without blocking half the harbor. He answers to the city, not to the merchant league.

Defense and commerce

City watch, river guards, and customs officers serve separate commands. Patrol launches pursue thieves and assist boats in trouble. Warehouse guards protect their employers' property, but do not possess general powers of arrest. Grain, salted food, timber, rope, imported cloth, and river transport dominate business. Public scales settle quantities, while licensed inspectors examine seals and cargo manifests. The Auren Navigation Accord provides procedures for disputes with Serath, but a delayed hearing can still cost a merchant an entire season.

Districts and landmarks

The Upper Ledger is the relatively dry commercial quarter of countinghouses and clerks' rooms. Below it, the Bargeward contains crowded boardinghouses and workshops. The Long Quay is a sequence of linked loading yards rather than one continuous street; gates and schedules can make a short distance surprisingly difficult to cross. The Accord House. Envoys and inspectors meet here over navigation disputes. Registrar Neris Val keeps copies of recognized dues and prior rulings. She can explain what a document says but will not guarantee that a foreign officer will agree with her interpretation. The Tally Exchange. Buyers bid on inspected consignments beneath numbered timber galleries. Exchange keeper Doran Pike, a halfling with a ringing auction voice, suspends traders who tamper with samples. The dispute board outside is one of the best places to find lawful paid work. The Flood Lanterns. A line of elevated lamps marks the safe evacuation route from the Bargeward. Families contribute oil each autumn. Several lamp towers also store ladders, blankets, and rescue lines.

Organizations and notable establishments

The Auren Factors. Speaker Mara Venn represents the wider league; Dunmere's resident factor is Joren Pellis, who keeps an office in the Tally Exchange. A league letter introduces a traveler; it does not exempt anyone from local law. The Riverhands' Table. Chairwoman Bessa Quill coordinates dock crews, injured workers' support, and disputes over wages. Her association can halt work in a particular yard, but cannot command every worker. Its morning meal is a dependable place to meet a captain looking for extra hands.

Primary faiths

Connections beyond the settlement

The Auren Factors maintain member countinghouses in Aurenhall and purchasing agents at Fairmead. The city

Fairmead stands northeast of Aurenhall amid productive farmland, connected to both northern routes and the river markets. Its skyline is a row of granary roofs and wind vanes rather than a palace. On market days, carts queue beyond the houses while drovers guide livestock through lanes built to keep them away from crowded food stalls. Population: approximately 18,500, rising sharply during harvest fairs. Farming families maintain rooms here for market visits; halfling seed merchants, human growers, and dwarven millwrights have particularly visible trade communities.

Government and leadership

Lady Warden Corren Tal holds the provincial commission. A former cavalry officer who now spends more time judging drainage disputes than reviewing troops, she favors practical agreements and dislikes exaggerated claims of ancient privilege. Fairmead's civic council chooses Market Reeve Orla Denwick, a human wool trader whose patience disappears when someone alters a posted fee. Corren handles crown obligations and serious criminal matters. Orla oversees stalls, municipal measures, refuse collection, and minor market offenses. Both claim the right to close unsafe storage buildings, a rare overlap on which they usually agree.

Defense and commerce

Paid guards are reinforced during fairs by hired constables and a civic reserve. Low walls and stout gates protect the storeyards rather than every house. Livestock lanes can be closed quickly during an alarm. Seed grain, wool, breeding animals, preserved food, and milling equipment dominate commerce. A visitor can buy excellent ordinary tools but must usually order specialized arms from a larger center. Market inspectors examine samples before opening each fair, though they cannot prevent every substituted sack or concealed defect.

Landmarks and useful places

The Ninefold Market. Nine adjoining yards separate grain, livestock, wool, tools, and other goods. Bellkeeper Toma Sedge coordinates opening times and lost-property announcements. The lost-child bench beside his tower is also where separated travelers arrange to meet. The Seedhouse. Keeper Ysol Bracken maintains a collection of viable seed varieties, planting records, and accounts of local crop failures. She follows Ilyra and works with ordinary farmers as readily as scholars. Samples are exchanged under written agreements so one wealthy buyer cannot remove a district's entire reserve. The Old Dry Court. This raised enclosure served as a flood refuge before the surrounding market expanded. Its original paving survives beneath a newer roof. Travelers sometimes mistake its unusual stones for waystone material; local masons distinguish the two and discourage visitors from testing either with a hammer.

Organizations

The Seasonal Keepers of Fairmead. Led by Ysol Bracken from the Seedhouse, this regional gathering of the wider Seasonal Keepers maintains experimental plots and organizes help for damaged farms. Its members disagree about unfamiliar crops, but share observations openly at the spring gathering. Their blessings supplement labor and good husbandry rather than replacing them. The Fair Scales Company. Master Assayer Bren Colwick directs an independent inspection partnership hired by both growers and buyers. Its office near the Ninefold Market displays calibrated measures. Bren, a meticulous gnome, refuses payment contingent on a favorable finding. His assistants often need protection when a large consignment fails inspection. The Drovers' Ring. Speaker Nella Voss organizes grazing stops and the recovery of strayed animals. She is unrelated to the senior Ledger broker Cassian Voss and is tired of being asked. The Ring arranges paid work for scouts, animal handlers, and patient people willing to walk behind cattle in the rain.

The Bent Sheaf

Proprietor Alden Marr, a retired cook with an expansive laugh, runs this inn around a wagon court. Private rooms are scarce during fairs, but the covered yard rents secure sleeping spaces. He keeps a board of agreed labor rates to discourage travelers from accepting obviously unfair harvest contracts.

Primary faiths

Ilyra - The Seedhouse Sanctuary. Ysol Bracken's gardens and assembly room form the city's principal temple. As a regional meeting place for the Seasonal Keepers, it exchanges seed, field reports, and teaching visits with Aurenhall and Hearthglen. Mereth - The Fairground Infirmary. A modest House of the Sheltered Drop treats market injuries and provides an isolation room when livestock fairs bring illness. Supplies come through Aurenhall; visiting carers also help nearby villages. Elsira and Leth - The Drovers' Hearth and Fork Shrine. These two small shrines occupy opposite sides of the same sheltered market court. Elsira's steward arranges emergency beds with local inns; a lay Companion of the Fork posts road news. Fairmead has fewer large temples than the river capitals, despite its busy fairs.

Connections beyond the settlement

The Fair Scales Company holds an inspection contract with the Granary Trust and sends assessors to Dunmere when consignments are disputed. The Drovers' Ring negotiates northern grazing stages with Northbridge's Pasture Moot. Ysol's Seasonal Keepers supply teaching visits and seed exchanges to Hearthglen through the western market roads.

Creatures & travel warnings

Outlying orchard families sometimes strengthen their stock gates against owlbears and unusually bold boars. Seed keepers and caravan drivers exchange practical reports about creatures moving out of the uplands during lean years.

Residents include bridge workers, herders, soldiers' families, merchants, and people with relatives on both sides of the northern frontier. National allegiance does not erase those relationships.

Government and leadership

Marchwarden Halen Vaust holds the crown's local military commission. He is a lean human veteran who dislikes raids, retaliatory raids, and speeches that make either sound inexpensive. The city charter gives civilian affairs to a borough council led by Speaker Meira Holt, a half-elf leather merchant with strong ties to northern drovers. Halen wants dependable patrols and clear reporting. Meira wants troops paid on time so military requisitions do not become informal confiscation. Their disagreements usually concern expense and jurisdiction rather than loyalty to the kingdom.

Defense and commerce

A civic watch serves the town while a separate crown garrison patrols the crossing and surrounding roads. Teren's chaplains assist the garrison but do not command it. Closing the reinforced bridge gates is possible, though costly to local trade. Livestock, hides, wool, grain, and horse trading sustain Northbridge. Repair shops specialize in tack, wagons, and weatherproof clothing. Travelers carrying animals should expect questions about brands, grazing agreements, and disease. Landmarks

Engineer Kessa Tor, a dwarf who carries a small notebook even to supper, closes lanes whenever inspection requires it despite loud objections from traders. The Watch of Seven Lamps. Organizations and lodging

Dwarven teamsters and merchants from the mountain roads mix with Aramere's farming families and Caldran carriers. The city is smaller than the great river markets but handles a disproportionate share of western traffic.

Government and leadership

Lady Magistrate Elowen Hart administers the crown's district commission from the Three Roads Hall. A human in her early fifties, she is courteous, exacting, and more impressed by a repaired culvert than a polished recommendation. Her jurisdiction includes Hearthglen and other dependent rural communities. The city elects a Yard Council to oversee markets and public services. Its current chair, dwarf wheelwright Brannik Vale, wants the new western-road investment spent on surfaces, drainage, and safe stopping places. He openly opposes prestigious gatehouses that do little for travelers.

Defense and commerce

The city watch patrols the yards and markets; district riders serve the surrounding roads and villages. Registered caravan guards may carry arms but remain subject to the magistrate's law. Haulage, storage, livestock fodder, repairs, and cargo transfer dominate trade. Khardun's metalwork moves toward the lowlands while food and timber travel toward the mountain markets. Caldran cloth, salt fish, and imported goods arrive through western connections. Buying a seat with a caravan is easier than hiring a private coach.

Landmarks and institutions

The Three Roads Hall. Magistrate Hart's court shares a building with the district registry and a public notice gallery. Clerk Nerin Shaw receives petitions from village reeves. He is an elderly halfling who writes slowly but will explain what supporting details a petition lacks. A sealed letter from Hearthglen receives attention; it does not guarantee an immediate escort. The Joined Yards. Three large caravan courts share a public well and repair lane. Yardmaster Maela Dorn assigns arrival spaces and mediates disputes over overnight obstruction. Different companies rent different courts, so there is no single master of every caravan. The Turning Stone. An ancient waystone stands beside a road junction outside the busiest yards. Locals leave a small wheeled toy there when a child first travels away from home. Its original purpose is unknown, and the custom is evidence of local affection, not an explanation of its making.

Organizations

The Westroad Carriers' Compact. Convenor Rulden Pike coordinates shared rescue equipment, notices of road damage, and compensation discussions among participating companies. Amra helps people find family or safe onward travel and refuses to reveal a guest's private affairs merely because a creditor asks. The Road Survey Office. Surveyor Lenna Dask leads a small crown-funded team evaluating the western routes. She needs local guides and records of seasonal washouts. Her appointment permits surveying public roads, not excavating private land or removing ancient objects.

The village and its country

Hearthglen lies in western Aramere, northeast of Threeways and northwest of Aurenhall. Its cottages gather around a broad green above a small working stream. Beyond them are grain fields, kitchen gardens, coppiced woodland, and orchards on the gentler slopes. On a cold morning, hearth smoke lingers low enough for a person on the western lane to smell breakfast before seeing the village. Most houses are timber framed on stone footings, with tile or thatch roofs according to household means. The central settlement has roughly sixty occupied homes and work buildings; many more residents live on surrounding farms. A low stone bridge carries the mill lane across the stream. No wall separates the village from the countryside. Population: approximately 690 including its dependent farms, with around 250 living in the central settlement. Human and halfling families are most numerous; a few dwarven, elven, gnome, and mixed households are well established. Seasonal laborers are usually lodged by the farms that employ them.

History and daily life

Hearthglen grew around a sheltered stopping place and a dependable mill site. Its oldest surviving rent rolls are early Accord-era copies of earlier claims. Several families disagree about whose ancestors first made the green common land. None has produced a document accepted by everyone. Hearthglen supplies the surrounding district rather than distant royal markets. It supplies grain, cider, cheese, wool, and finished wood to the markets around Threeways. Peddlers bring news, salt, needles, lamp oil, and tools too specialized to make locally. A well-equipped stranger is interesting, but travelers are familiar enough that their arrival does not stop all work.

Government and leadership

Reeve Mara Bellweather heads a five-member village council, chosen by household representatives at the winter meeting. Mara is a human woman in her late forties, a former teamster with wind-reddened cheeks and the habit of checking every knot herself. She manages the communal granary, records local dues, and carries petitions to Threeways. The council handles common land, minor disputes, and maintenance. Serious crimes and contested land titles go to Lady Magistrate Elowen Hart. Hearthglen pays district dues but has no resident noble household. Mara can hire help within a modest emergency allowance; she cannot grant titles, waive royal taxes, or promise money the village does not possess.

Defense

Constable Jory Kest and two paid assistants investigate theft, keep order at markets, and organize alarms. A rotating village reserve assists them, but most members are usually working fields or trades. A sustained emergency requires help from Threeways. Jory, a human former district rider with a stiff left shoulder, knows this and avoids presenting the militia as a professional army. The green's bell uses distinct signals for fire, missing persons, and immediate danger. Families have assigned meeting places during an alarm. The mill loft and refuge cellar hold basic emergency supplies. The village sometimes employs travelers for searches, difficult escorts, or dangers its residents cannot safely handle.

Faith and custom

Ilyra receives the largest public observances, especially planting and harvest. Mereth has a small treatment room, while Elsira is honored at the inn and refuge. Sovan's remembrance rites are held at the hillside burial ground. People routinely attend more than one observance. At the First Loaf supper, every household contributes something to the public table or the winter store. The ceremonial loaf is deliberately uneven: each portion represents a different household, and no one is supposed to complain about the size they receive. Complaints are nevertheless a reliable part of the evening.

The Copper Kettle

Proprietor: Tamsin Alder, a halfling woman with silver-threaded hair, brisk manners, and a gift for remembering who arrived hungry. Her two-story inn has six guest rooms, a common-room hearth, a stable for eight riding animals, and covered wagon space. Overflow guests can rent a pallet when traveling workers fill the rooms. Tamsin serves bean stew, onion bread, orchard preserves, and excellent cider. She sets aside supper for a guest who cannot pay, honoring Elsira without making a spectacle of charity. The inn is Hearthglen's public meeting place, informal hiring hall, and the best place to hear several incompatible versions of the same event.

Bellweather Stores

Keeper: Halven Bellweather, the reeve's younger brother. This modest general shop sells food staples, rope, candles, blankets, hand tools, and traveling supplies. Halven keeps his accounts separate from Mara's civic ledger and becomes defensive when anyone suggests otherwise. He can order unusual goods through Threeways but cannot promise immediate delivery.

The Ember and Iron

Smith: Dorrin Ashmantle, a dwarf who moved to the village after years repairing caravan equipment. His forge produces nails, hinges, blades for farm work, horseshoes, and sturdy simple weapons. He repairs ordinary armor; a complicated commission needs materials and time. He reveres Teren through service and offers militia equipment repairs at cost.

The Four Seasons House

Keeper: Sister Elira Moss, a human Ilyran priest with soil usually caught beneath her nails. The timber temple opens onto a seed garden and covered gathering yard. Elira teaches planting customs, keeps local weather notes, and organizes shared labor after crop damage. She refuses to promise divine favor in exchange for the largest donation.

The Willow Room

Healer: Nerissa Holt, a half-elf herbalist and lay follower of Mereth. Her two-bed treatment room adjoins the temple yard but has its own entrance. She treats ordinary injuries and illness, recognizes when a patient needs help she cannot provide, and arranges transport toward Threeways. Medicines and prepared supplies are limited; requests for powerful magic must be referred elsewhere.

The Lantern House

Steward: Pell Underbough, an elderly halfling widower and member of the Fellowship of Open Doors. A small communal refuge, it has eight ordinary beds and room for more during an emergency. Pell helps stranded laborers, displaced families, and travelers who cannot manage the inn's prices. Mother Hessa Vale's network supplies occasional aid, not an unlimited purse.

The Turning Mill

The Witness Court

Within the court's original stone boundary, people cannot knowingly speak a falsehood. They may remain silent, use narrow wording, or honestly repeat a mistake. The place does not read thoughts, compel answers, prove an inference, or force someone to honor a promise after leaving. Custodians identify the recognized boundary and set hearing procedures. Public readers repeat statements for assembled witnesses; trained questioners help expose omissions. An unprepared visitor can still enter a deeply unfair agreement using entirely truthful words. No ordinary spell or claimed extension of the paving is treated as equivalent to the ancient court.

Defense and commerce

The city watch and the custodians' precinct wardens cooperate at public entrances under agreed procedures. Foreign bodyguards remain subject to local law. A hearing at the Witness Court does not put a visitor beyond the city's jurisdiction. Document copying, translation, lodging, arbitration, banking, and ordinary trade support Veycross. Reliable witnesses and competent interpreters are valuable. Fees vary; the city has no single tariff that makes every legal service affordable.

Aramere — The River Kingdom

Land and character

Aramere occupies the central and south-central basin east of the lower Crownspine. Broad fields, river terraces, orchards, and pasture support one of Selarin's largest populations. Its wealth is conspicuous at harvest: queues of loaded wagons, barges riding low in the water, and public granaries whose doors bear several different seals. The river plain has warm summers and cool winters. Spring flooding is an expected burden, made dangerous by neglected banks or unusual Breathweather. Western uplands are less fertile but give access to Khardun and Caldris. Villages there often feel more connected to a market road than to the royal capital.

Government and the crown

King Edric Vaun rules from Aurenhall. The River Compact of 287 AR bound older principalities to common responsibilities for granaries, navigation, and flood works. Provincial houses retain substantial local authority, while major towns expect a voice in the River Council. The king's legitimacy rests partly on ensuring that these obligations are met. Edric is deliberate in public and impatient in working meetings. He wants western roads improved so Aramere can trade without depending entirely on Serathian customs houses. He seeks surveyors, reliable reports, and officials who can finish a bridge without turning it into a quarrel over precedence.

The River Council

Seat: the Compact House in Aurenhall. First Speaker: Lady Ysabet Rennel, a provincial landholder known for making ministers answer the same question until they give a usable answer. The council negotiates reserve targets, navigation rules, and shared expenditure. Its votes do not automatically settle every local dispute: provincial officers must still carry decisions into effect. Ysabet supports the western-road policy but demands that poorer districts receive maintenance money as well as new construction. Petitioners normally approach through a town delegate or provincial clerk. Testimony from a village can matter if someone has properly recorded it.

The Granary Trust

Warden-General: Tomas Reed, based at the Seven Granaries in Aurenhall. This public institution supervises crown reserves and inspects participating town stores. Local keepers retain their keys; inventories and releases require witnesses from more than one office. The Trust purchases grain, pays carriers, and calls on local authorities for protection. Tomas distrusts impressive totals that cannot be matched to actual sacks. His inspectors employ escorts, assess storm damage, and investigate claims of spoiled reserves. They can request records but cannot confiscate a family's harvest merely by showing a badge.

The Bankwardens

Chief Warden: Della Marr, a dwarf engineer whose office travels between flood districts. The Bankwardens maintain gauges, embankments, channels, and emergency plans with local labor crews. Their authority concerns public waterworks, not every stream or millpond in the kingdom. Farmers respect their flood warnings and argue fiercely over who supplies the workers.

Faith and ordinary life

Ilyra's seasonal keepers advise on planting and soil; Mereth's healers tend crowded markets and isolated farms. Elsiran houses turn hospitality into dependable shelter. A household may honor all three. Festivals often include a useful obligation: filling a reserve bin, clearing a ditch, or delivering food to someone who cannot attend. The Grain War with Serath, 603-607 AR, remains a warning about the cost of disrupted trade. Today's Auren Navigation Accord supports peace, but inspections, damaged cargo, and disputed dues still produce work for advocates and escorts. Aramere's people usually fear a failed harvest before they fear an invading army.

Primary faiths

Ilyra, Mereth, and Elsira are the most widely visible faiths. Seasonal Keepers exchange seed and weather records between Aurenhall, Fairmead, and rural houses. Mereth's urban hospices supply smaller treatment rooms through traveling carers. Elsiran refuges share news through the Fellowship of Open Doors. Teren is prominent along guarded crossings, while Ordan and Veyra are honored wherever waterworks and agreements need keeping.

Regional creatures & dangers

On the settled river roads, danger is less frequent than at the edges of the basin. Owlbears and dire wolves are reported in wooded uplands when prey runs scarce, while great boars and giant spiders trouble isolated coppices and neglected sheds. Marsh channels sometimes shelter large serpents or other aggressive water creatures; boatmen mark suspect banks and avoid landing there after dark. Old workings near waystones can present stranger hazards, especially during unsettled Breathweather. Most villages treat such threats as practical problems. Watch bells, reinforced pens, experienced guides, and shared sightings usually matter more than extravagant claims of a curse. Travelers should ask a reeve, ferryman, or carrier for recent reports instead of assuming every forest is unsafe.

Caldris — The Chartered Crown

Land and character

Caldris lies west and southwest of the Crownspine and reaches around its southern end. Coastal farms, wooded uplands, market towns, and sea traffic give the kingdom several sources of wealth. Its mild coast contrasts with colder inland heights. Winter rain can make low roads difficult even when the sea remains navigable. The kingdom consolidated in 204 AR around coastal lords and chartered markets. Its identity rests on negotiated rights: who may hold a fair, collect a toll, maintain a crossing, or bring a case before a particular court. These questions affect ordinary households as much as nobles.

Queen Marwen Calder

Marwen governs from Caer Calden. A thoughtful human ruler with a restrained public manner, she favors common law, dependable roads, and predictable dues. She rarely announces a major policy before securing enough support to make it workable. Critics call this hesitation; allies call it remembering that a proclamation cannot repair a bridge. Landed houses retain inherited jurisdictions, while chartered towns resist demands that ignore their privileges. The queen must negotiate taxation and public works with both. Her strongest supporters hope commerce will make cooperation more attractive than repeated local quarrels.

The Crown Road Office

Royal Surveyor Sered Aln directs the office from Caer Calden. Its clerks document lawful tolls, inspect crown-supported bridges, and coordinate contracts across local boundaries. Sered, a patient dwarf engineer, wants consistent records that survive the retirement of the official who remembers why a fee exists. The office employs surveyors, messengers, escorts, and independent witnesses. Its commissions specify where its authority applies; a crown badge is not permission to enter any estate or ignore a town charter. Disputes over the office's reach often matter as much as the damaged road itself.

The Charter Assembly

Speaker Alys Ferrow convenes representatives of participating towns when the crown requests common contributions or changes affecting their rights. It is an assembly of interests, not a parliament governing every part of the kingdom. Alys comes from Caer Calden's cloth trade and has a talent for presenting a united answer after very loud private disagreements. The assembly supports road improvements while demanding clear accounts. Smaller towns worry that ports and the capital will capture the benefits. Its agents seek local testimony, especially when a proposed toll would shift traffic from one market to another.

The Southern Road Company

Director Merovan Saye leads a chartered commercial concern with offices in Caer Calden and Veycross. It arranges freight, secure storage, and caravan services along the routes toward Aramere. Its charter grants specified rights, not exclusive ownership of every road. Independent carriers regularly challenge its interpretation of favorable clauses. Merovan is a polished human negotiator who values reputation because it makes future contracts cheaper. He hires practical escorts and investigators, but prefers a quiet settlement to a public victory that frightens customers.

Faith, borders, and daily life

Avariel's judges and Veyra's record keepers are influential, especially in Veycross. Elsiran inns and local shrines serve travelers throughout the kingdom. Coastal households honor Thessar before voyages, while inland growers keep Ilyra's seasonal customs. The Woodland Articles of 558 AR recognized some protected forests along Elaran's southern edge without resolving every settlement claim. Near Bellford, a new cutting road may therefore be an economic opportunity to one community and a broken inherited promise to another. Caldris also competes with Aramere for western trade while relying on cooperation to keep it moving. Neither border is a simple line between friends and enemies.

Primary faiths

Avariel and Veyra have influential public institutions in the capital and Veycross. The Uncrowned Bench's advocates travel to chartered towns, while independent Veyran readers preserve local records. Ilyra's keepers serve the inland farms and woodland districts. Thessar's coastal shrines, Elsira's houses, and Leth's roadside altars are familiar to travelers. Nivara's Open Song links performers from Bellford to the capital and port.

Regional creatures & dangers

The coast and chartered roads are generally traveled safely, but inland woods, neglected bridges, and steep ravines offer shelter to trolls, great wolves, and dangerous hunting beasts. Wyverns have been reported over the rough western reaches; old holdings sometimes attract grave-robbers, restless dead, or guardians left behind by earlier masters. Offshore, unusual sea creatures and storm-tossed wreck sites present risks familiar to experienced pilots. Travelers should distinguish rumor from a recent account. Charter towns post notices about closed bridges and troublesome creatures, and coastal watch captains collect reports of unfamiliar shapes in the water. A guide's local knowledge is usually more valuable than a dramatic tavern tale. Queen Marwen Calder Marwen governs from Caer Calden. A thoughtful human ruler with a restrained public manner, she favors common law, dependable roads, and predictable dues. She rarely announces a major policy before securing enough support to make it workable. Critics call this hesitation; allies call it remembering that a proclamation cannot repair a bridge. Landed houses retain inherited jurisdictions, while chartered towns resist demands that ignore their privileges. The queen must negotiate taxation and public works with both. Her strongest supporters hope commerce will make cooperation more attractive than repeated local quarrels.

Powers Across Borders — Institutions and Influence

The Custodians of the Witness Court

Leader: First Witness Sabren Holt. Seat: Veycross. Reach: diplomatic and religious connections across Selarin. The custodians maintain access, hearing procedures, and records associated with the ancient court. Their credibility depends on refusing to become simply an instrument of the Caldran crown. Their resources include precinct wardens, archives, readers, and experienced questioners. They do not possess an army able to enforce every agreement made before them. A commission may involve escorting witnesses, recovering a copy of a proceeding, or identifying what a poorly framed question failed to establish.

The Fellowship of Open Doors

Coordinator: Mother Hessa Vale. Bases: a network of Elsiran refuges rather than one controlling headquarters. Local contacts include Amra Cole in Threeways and Pell Underbough in Hearthglen. Houses share warnings, arrange shelter, and help separated families find one another while retaining considerable independence. Hessa is known for remembering the practical details of a case after officials have reduced it to a political problem. The Fellowship can provide introductions, beds when available, and trustworthy local knowledge. It cannot guarantee immunity from every lawful inquiry. Its willingness to shelter people desired by creditors or rulers can still become a serious dispute.

The Auren Factors

Representative: Speaker Mara Venn. Bases: merchant countinghouses along the Auren, with strong connections in Dunmere. This league favors predictable duties, navigable channels, and peace between Aramere and Serath. Members remain separate businesses and may compete fiercely over the same cargo. The Factors possess commercial information, warehouses, barges, and bargaining power. They hire escorts, auditors, translators, and negotiators. Their concern for stable trade can align with public welfare, but a profitable settlement may still leave a small supplier carrying most of the loss.

The Ledger Houses

Senior broker: Cassian Voss. Bases: independent houses linked through Cevrane's religious and financial institutions. Demea Sol represents the Veycross house. They finance shipments, property, and political projects through terms designed to preserve their influence. Their agents work openly where local law allows. A Ledger claim is not automatically valid in every court, and an ordinary modern loan is not automatically a supernatural bond. Their influence rests on resources, carefully worded contracts, and enduring commercial relationships. Independent advocates and rival lenders remain important checks.

The Collegium of Surviving Works

Leader: Provost Istran Pell, based in Serathis. Local correspondent: survey scholar Maerin Doss, who rents rooms in Threeways while examining western reports. The Collegium documents ancient structures and sponsors expeditions, but its members disagree about patronage and excavation rights. Maerin is a half-elf researcher who asks permission before taking measurements and is frustrated by colleagues who do not. A Collegium letter establishes an affiliation, not ownership of a site. Employment can include guiding a survey, making accurate drawings, protecting records, or finding people who remember earlier repairs. Modern scholarship has not recovered general control of the greatest ancient works.

The Road and River Accord Circle

Convenor: Advocate Sella Dain, based in Veycross with regular visits to Aurenhall. This new local association brings together mediators, clerks, and carriers interested in avoiding small commercial disputes becoming national grievances. It is not a treaty organization and cannot issue binding orders. Sella is a human advocate with an untidy satchel and a talent for asking what each side actually needs. The Circle commissions cross-border inquiries and introduces witnesses to mediators. Its funds are modest, and its influence depends on the trust of the people involved.

Beyond the royal courts

Orders, merchants & shared roads

Some institutions outlast treaties and speak across borders. Learn which names turn up at markets, in shrines, and on the road.

The Custodians of the Witness Court

First Witness Sabren Holt represents Veycross's custodians in dealings with rulers and foreign envoys. The office protects access to the court and maintains records of public proceedings. Its authority depends on trust across borders, so the custodians resist being treated as an instrument of Caldris. Their questioners and archives make them useful to anyone trying to establish what was actually promised.

The Fellowship of Open Doors

Mother Hessa Vale coordinates a network of Elsiran refuges and traveling stewards. Its houses arrange shelter, carry news about dangerous roads, and help displaced people locate relatives. Local houses retain considerable independence. Their willingness to shelter people whom a ruler wants returned can turn a charitable institution into an unexpected diplomatic force.

The Collegium of Surviving Works

Provost Istran Pell leads a Serathis-based association of scholars, surveyors, and practitioners with correspondents across the realms. It studies ancient structures and supports expeditions to document them. Its members disagree over excavation rights, public access to discoveries, and the demands of wealthy patrons. Provincial rulers welcome useful knowledge while watching for attempts to carry their inheritance abroad.

The Auren Factors and the Ledger Houses

Speaker Mara Venn represents a league of merchants whose warehouses and barges connect Aramere with Serath. The Auren Factors favor predictable duties and navigable rivers, giving them reasons to pressure both governments toward peace. They are commercial partners rather than a single sovereign power. Cevrane's Ledger Houses finance ventures across the same routes. A senior broker active along these routes, Cassian Voss, offers courts and merchants access to credit at terms carefully designed to preserve his institutions' influence. Their contracts are public business, even where their theology is unwelcome. Between crowns, temples, and lenders, a modest bargain can acquire consequences that reach far beyond the town where it was made.

Roads, rivers, and fellowships directory

Institutions with a shared reach

Aramere's Granary Trust and Bankwardens work outward from Aurenhall through city officers, visiting inspectors, and local agreements. Neither institution owns every village store or watercourse. Threeways is their western point of contact; Fairmead organizes harvest traffic; Dunmere and Northbridge report river and crossing conditions. Caldris's Crown Road Office operates from Caer Calden, with correspondents in Veycross and Westhaven and a desk in Bellford. The Charter Assembly brings those towns into common negotiations. Southern Road Company offices link the capital, Veycross, and the port, while independent carriers remain active.

Faiths that connect communities

The Houses of the Sheltered Drop share medical knowledge and supplies. In Aramere, Aurenhall supports Dunmere, Fairmead, and Threeways; Threeways supplies Nerissa Holt in Hearthglen. Caldris's Calden Hospice cooperates with houses in Veycross and Westhaven. The Fellowship of Open Doors links refuges across both kingdoms under Mother Hessa Vale's coordination. Amra Cole at Threeways supports Pell Underbough at Hearthglen. Local houses remain independent households with their own resources and customs. The Seasonal Keepers share records and seed rather than obeying a single high priest. Aurenhall and Fairmead are important Aramere meeting places; Elira Moss exchanges reports from Hearthglen. Bellford's keepers maintain their own connections with farming and woodland communities. The Uncrowned Bench trains advocates in Caer Calden and Veycross and sends visitors to Bellford and Westhaven. Teren's Wardens of the Seven Watches link Northbridge, Threeways, and the mountain roads. Nivara's Open Song connects Bellford's school, Calden's Lantern Stage, and Veycross's Courtyard of Voices through visiting performers.

Traveling with an introduction

A temple letter can establish who tended you or what help you seek. A carrier's reference can describe your work. A town seal can confirm who authorized a petition. None promises that the next person has the beds, money, or authority to grant everything requested, but each gives that person a reason to begin the conversation.

A carrier's introduction may also name a trusted guide, an inn with secure animal pens, or a keeper who maintains the latest reports of threats along a remote road.
