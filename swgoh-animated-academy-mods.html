Mod Improvement Strategy

A repeatable process for turning a crowded mod inventory into a stronger roster: reveal cheaply, stop bad investments early, preserve rare exceptions, and spend slicing materials only on pieces that still have upside.

The repeatable loop

Seven-step improvement funnel

This keeps credits, slicing material, and calibration material focused on mods that have earned the next investment.

Protect what already matters

Save important loadouts or otherwise document your best competitive teams. Remod your highest-priority characters before culling inventory so good but poorly assigned pieces are not mistaken for junk.

Remove obvious dead weight

Sell 1–4 dot mods once your account is established on 5-dot gear. Clear true duplicates and low-value pieces that have no rare primary, no useful stat profile, and no intentional slow-use case.

Reveal all secondaries as cheaply as possible

Do not level every new mod straight to 15. Reveal all four secondaries first. Practical reveal stopping points are level 3 for purple, 6 for blue, 9 for green, and 12 for grey mods.

Use speed as the default filter

For most characters, a speed secondary is the best general indicator of future value. Keep promising speed pieces moving through the funnel, but route no-speed pieces through the exception check before selling.

Roll only mods that still have a job

Continue leveling or slicing when the piece has useful stat synergy and still has chances to improve. Stop when multiple rolls miss the stats that make the mod valuable.

Reserve 6-dot investment for proven pieces

Take 5A mods to 6E when the primary/set is valuable and the secondary profile already justifies a more expensive investment. Prioritize relic characters and mods with strong speed or clearly synergistic non-speed stats.

Calibrate late, not early

Calibration materials are scarce. Use them on strong 6-dot mods where moving a bad roll can realistically turn an already useful mod into an elite one. Do not use calibration to rescue fundamentally weak pieces.

Fast decisions

What to sell, hold, or invest in

The goal is consistent triage, not a rigid rule that throws away valuable edge cases.

Usually sellLow-dot or directionless

1–4 dot mods; 5-dot pieces with weak primary/set fit, no speed, and no other compelling stat synergy; heavily rolled mods that repeatedly missed the stats they needed.

Hold / developUseful but unfinished

Fresh 5-dot mods with speed revealed; rare primary/set combinations; mods with strong offense, health, protection, potency, tenacity, or defense synergy even if speed is modest.

InvestProven upside

Mods with repeated speed hits, premium primary/set combinations, multiple useful secondaries, or a clear assignment to a high-priority character.

Do not mass-delete “no speed” mods without review. Speed arrows, intentional slow characters, turn-order strategies, and rare primaries can make a no-speed-secondary mod exactly what you need.
Speed quality

Use bands, not a single magic number

A low current speed value can still be a good candidate when a mod is young. Judge both present speed and how many future rolls remain.

Current speed secondaryHow to treat itTypical use
0Exception check before sellingSlow builds, speed-primary arrows, rare primaries, very strong non-speed synergy
3–7Development candidate if the mod is still youngEarly reveal stage; keep when many roll opportunities remain
8–11ServiceableUseful depth pieces, especially with good complementary stats
12–14GoodWorth keeping on a broad roster; often worth further investment
15–19StrongHigh-priority teams and many 6-dot candidates
20+PremiumProtect, assign carefully, and consider calibration only if the rest of the mod supports it
Roll count matters: one speed hit is a candidate, two is useful, three or more is increasingly valuable. The same +5 speed can mean “promising” on a fresh mod and “disappointing” on a mod that has already burned most of its chances.

Avoid expensive mistakes

Exception checklist

These are the pieces most likely to be destroyed by an overly aggressive “speed or sell” rule.

Intentional slow builds

Some characters or team orders work better when a unit moves late. Preserve mods created for deliberate sequencing rather than forcing speed everywhere.

Speed-primary arrows

A speed arrow already gets speed from its primary. It cannot be judged by the absence of a speed secondary in the same way as other shapes.

Rare primary + set combinations

Critical-damage or offense triangles, potency/tenacity crosses, and specialized tank primaries can be worth holding even with mediocre speed because replacing the combination may be difficult.

Stat-synergy specialists

Attackers may want offense/crit stats, tanks health/protection/defense, and debuff-focused units potency. A coherent stat package can outperform a random speed-only piece.

Turn-order mods

Sometimes the exact relative speeds of two allies matter more than each individual speed total. Keep pieces that help maintain a tested sequence.

Roster depth

Do not apply endgame thresholds to a roster that still needs broad coverage. Replace weak equipped mods before selling every “merely average” piece.

Consensus behind the strategy

Across long-running mod guides and newer creator tutorials, the recurring pattern is consistent: speed is the default secondary to pursue; reveal before fully upgrading; stop spending when rolls go bad; slice selectively; and do not let a simple speed rule override character-specific needs. HotUtils is best used to apply those rules across the inventory in batches rather than as a substitute for understanding why a mod is valuable.

This alliance workflow is presented as a standalone mod-management process. Use HotUtils or the in-game mod tools to apply the steps and track your cleanup passes.
